What the research says about the internet, focus and mood

Last updated: November 14, 2026

Studies show that less time online tends to mean better attention and a better mood. In one 2025 trial, 91% of people who blocked the internet on their phones for two weeks improved. Here are the key studies in plain words, with links to every original paper.

These studies looked at phones, social media and laptops, not at Vow. They help explain why less internet often means more focus and a better mood. We’ve kept each one in plain words and linked the original.

Attention and focus

91%

467 adults blocked the internet on their phones for two weeks. 91% got better in attention, mental health or well-being. Their attention improved by about as much as it normally declines over 10 years of ageing.[1]

47 sec

How long people stayed on one screen, on average, before switching, across studies from 2016 to 2020. In 2004 it was about two and a half minutes.[2]

Even off

Just having your own phone on the desk, switched off and face down, lowered scores on memory and thinking tests. People did best with the phone in another room.[3]

Lower scores

Students who multitasked on a laptop during a lecture scored lower on a test afterwards, and so did students who could see their screens.[4]

Mood and well-being

4 weeks

People who turned off Facebook for four weeks spent more time offline, felt a bit happier, and kept using it less afterwards.[5]

10 min

Students who limited Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to 10 minutes each a day for three weeks felt less lonely and less depressed.[6]

Colleges

When Facebook arrived at US colleges, students’ mental health got worse, especially depression. Researchers linked it to comparing themselves with others.[7]

Stress

People who scroll bad news more also report more stress and anxiety and lower well-being. This is a link, not proof of cause.[8]

Screens and ADHD symptoms

+10% odds

For each extra kind of digital media teens used many times a day, their odds of new ADHD symptoms over two years rose by about 10%. A link, not proof that screens cause ADHD.[9]

What this means for you

You don’t have to quit the internet. The studies point to something simpler: less of it, at the right times, helps. Pick the hours that matter, like study time, deep work or late at night, and make them offline. Guides: how to stay focused on a Mac, social media addiction, ADHD focus on a Mac.
